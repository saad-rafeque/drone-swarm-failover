Swarm Failover

A group of quadcopters that keeps flying its mission when the leader fails. Every drone runs the same code. If the leader crashes, loses its radio or runs low on battery, the next drone takes over within about two seconds and the swarm carries on to the target.

Why build it

In many drone groups one leader, or one ground station, makes the decisions. When it fails, the whole mission fails with it. This project removes that single point of failure. There is no central controller: each drone decides its own role from the messages it hears, so the group survives the loss of any member, including the leader.

Failures are expensive to test on real aircraft, so everything is built and proven in simulation first, with the same code that will later run on the drones. The simulator doubles as a training ground for reinforcement learning, which is the research goal of the project.

Civilian uses: search and rescue over large areas, flood and disaster mapping, crop surveys, power-line and pipeline inspection, and light shows. Weapons, payloads and targeting are out of scope.

How it works

Each drone carries three layers. The swarm agent (the project's code) chooses the leader, keeps the drone in its slot of the V, and enforces the safety rules: at least 5 m between drones and a geofence. MAVROS translates its commands into MAVLink, the standard drone protocol, and the PX4 autopilot flies the aircraft. The agents talk to each other only through a small heartbeat message broadcast five times a second.

Choosing a leader

The lowest-numbered healthy drone leads. If nobody hears the leader for 1.5 s, the next one claims the job with a higher “term” number, so after any radio split heals there is exactly one leader. A leader with a low battery hands over on purpose and flies home.

Keeping formation

Followers take slots on two arms of a V, 10 m apart, turned to the leader’s heading. Each follows the leader’s velocity plus a correction toward its slot, and a repulsion term pushes drones apart when any two come within 7.5 m.

City to city, with charging stops

A small quadcopter flies only a few kilometres per battery, so long routes are planned with charging stops about every 4 km (half a battery per leg, a 3-minute battery swap), and the building map is downloaded as a strip along the route. Islamabad (F-9 Park) to Rawalpindi Saddar, 12 km: all 10 drones landed at the target with no hit after two stops. Islamabad to Lahore, 272.55 km with 65 stops: all 10 drones landed in Lahore after 18 simulated hours with no hit. Once, after a charging stop, a tall building held one follower back for about two minutes before it caught up. Flying low (16 m) through town is still hard: on the 12 km route three followers got stuck at a wall and hit it.

Reinforcement learning: first results

The learned part is local obstacle avoidance: how each follower dodges buildings and trees while it keeps its slot in the V. The leader flies a route planned around the buildings (A*), and the brake and the 5 m separation rule stay on as hard safety layers. Every drone runs the same small network (two layers of 128), trained with PPO for 6 million steps in 38 minutes on the laptop CPU.

All five methods flew the same 90 routes that neither training nor model selection had seen, 30 at each obstacle density, 10 drones each. A mission counts as successful when no drone hits anything and the whole V re-forms at the target.

What longer training (for example on Kaggle) should improve

  • More steps: held-out success was still rising at the end of this run (5 of 8 medium-density missions at 6 million steps). Planned: 20–50 million.
  • Several seeds, so a result is not one lucky run; a paper-grade claim needs 3–5.
  • Training on real OpenStreetMap neighbourhoods instead of random boxes and tree clusters, the likely cause of the gap on the real map.
  • The brake inside training, so the policy learns to work with it instead of meeting it only at test time.
  • What it will not fix: the simplified physics, two-dimensional obstacles, and perfect range sensing.

    A small companion computer on each drone wired to its Pixhawk 6C, a radio link for heartbeats, a bench test with propellers off, then one real drone with nine simulated ones, then three real drones in an open field with safety pilots.

Known limitations

  • No real drone has flown this code yet. The Pixhawk 6C cannot run it alone; each drone needs a companion computer.
  • When the radio splits the swarm into groups whose drones are mixed together in the air, or random links drop, drones that cannot hear each other came closer than 5 m in 143 of 1,000 random runs of the fast simulator. This must be fixed before real flight.
  • Simulated GPS is near perfect. Real GPS drifts by metres, so real flights need RTK GPS or wider spacing.
  • The fast simulator uses simplified physics; PX4 simulation uses the real autopilot with simple aircraft physics and no real wind.
  • Heartbeats go from every drone to every drone, so radio load grows with swarm size.

How it was built

Python 3.12, ROS 2 Jazzy, MAVROS 2.15 and PX4 v1.18 (release candidate) in its built-in simulator, on one laptop without a GPU. The work follows seven phases, each with written pass/fail criteria, saved logs and a clean re-run before it counts. Every result above comes from logged test runs.

Evidence in the project repository: reports/PHASE_0.md to PHASE_2.md, reports/logs/phase_3/, reports/logs/scaling/. Sources: PX4 documentation (docs.px4.io) and PX4 source for system IDs; ArduPilot SiK radio documentation for air rates.
